 The ordering rule

 Everything reversible happens before anything irreversible, and the export happens before all of it.
 Step 7 should sit weeks after step 6, not hours — the WordPress host running untouched is the entire
 rollback plan.

 11.2 The export that cannot be undone
 do first 

 The new application and the old store share only a domain name. No data moves between
 them. When the hosting is cancelled, whatever was in WordPress is gone.

 What to export before anything else

 Customers — including anyone who registered but never ordered.

 Orders and their line items, whatever the store shows.

 Payment records from SureCart, including any partial or abandoned transactions.

 Products, so the withdrawn ones are documented as having existed.

 The full page and post content, in case a redirect decision needs reversing.

 The media library.

 Keep it somewhere durable in the company's name and record what was exported and when. If anyone
 ever asks what was sold before launch — a regulator, an insurer, an acquirer — this export is the
 only answer that will exist.

 11.3 What breaks the moment the domain moves
 plan for it 

 Four things, all foreseeable

 Every indexed URL. Handled by the redirect map in Chapter 1 — 301 where an equivalent exists, 410 where the thing is genuinely gone.

 Existing logins. Anyone with a WordPress account cannot get in, and there is no migration path. They need telling before, with a plain explanation and a route to their order history.

 Selling stops. Accepted deliberately: no rail is configured, so the site becomes a catalogue until Chapter 6 lands. Worth turning on bank transfer and BTCPay first so it is not literally zero.

 Search ranking. The old title targets a query the new site deliberately abandons. Record it as an accepted consequence so nobody treats it as a fault in three weeks.

 11.4 The cold-cache precondition is withdrawn
 corrected 12 Sep · Correction 5 

 Revision 1 of this chapter said a cold page load took 78 seconds and made fixing it a
 precondition for the cutover. That number was produced by the measuring tool, not by the site.

 What the second vantage showed

 Measured again with curl from a different machine: the document arrives in
 2.2 s cold and 1.2 s warm , and all 36 JavaScript chunks fetched concurrently and
 cache-busted complete in 2.07 s (slowest 1.3 s, median 1.0 s). Assets are
 served by Workers Assets with edge cache hits and a one-year immutable cache. The 21–23 s per-chunk
 stalls reproduced only inside the browser pane the first measurement was taken in.

 What still applies to the cutover

 A new domain is still a cold edge cache, so the first visitor to each page pays the 2 s render once.
 That is ordinary. Warming the top twenty paths right after the switch is cheap and worth doing, and the
 11 September 1102 errors (Worker CPU, see hub F-08) should be understood and the Workers plan
 confirmed before the date is set. Neither holds the cutover.

 Rule carried into every later chapter: no timing measured from a single vantage goes into a register.

 11.5 Rollback, and how to know you need it
 prepare 

 The plan is one DNS record

 Point the root back at the WordPress host. That works only while the host is still running and
 unchanged, which is why step 7 sits weeks after step 6.

 Decide the triggers before the day, not during it

 What page-load time is unacceptable, and who is watching it?

 What error rate triggers a rollback rather than a fix-forward?

 Who can make the call, at what hour, without waiting for anyone else? This is technical launch readiness — Ammre's veto — not a member decision.

 Mail is the thing to watch in the first hour: send from each mailbox to an outside address and back.
