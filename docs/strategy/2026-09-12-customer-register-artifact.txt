 10.1 What a customer profile is here — and the two things it must never hold
 define first 

 In most businesses a customer profile is a commercial object and the only question is how much to put
 in it. Here it is also a piece of evidence, and what you choose to record becomes a record of what you
 knew. That makes the exclusions as important as the contents, and they should be written down before
 anyone builds a field.

 What belongs in it

 Identity as stated: name, email, the research context they gave, their tier.

 The attestation record: which version of the research-use affirmation they accepted, and when.

 Every order, every line, and the specific lot each line shipped from.

 Addresses used, and whether each was residential or commercial.

 Documents issued to them: invoices, packing slips, the COA for each lot they received.

 Every interaction: support threads, complaints, returns, refunds — with who handled it.

 What must never go in it

 Purpose in any therapeutic sense. No condition field, no outcome dropdown, no "what are you researching" that invites a disease name. A free-text box where customers volunteer this is the same problem with extra steps — if you keep one, it needs a stated rule about what gets recorded and what gets discarded.

 Anything you inferred about use. "Reorders every 4 weeks" is a commercial fact. "Likely self-administering" is a conclusion, and writing it down turns a suspicion into a document a plaintiff can quote. Record behaviour; do not record theories about behaviour.

 Recommendation

 Write a one-page profile data policy naming every field, why it exists and how long it is kept, and put the two exclusions above at the top of it. Fatima should own it as the person who will actually be typing notes into customer records, and it should be the thing that gets pointed at when someone asks "can I just add a field for…".

 10.3 Purchase history, and the lot link that makes it worth more than a receipt list
 surface it 

 The data to show a customer every order, every line and the exact lot each vial came from is already
 there. Nobody in this category shows that, and for a researcher it is the difference between a shop
 and a supplier — it means a year later they can reconstruct exactly which certificate applies to the
 material in an experiment they are writing up.

 One real decision

 Lot movements are append-only and deliberately never exposed publicly — the lookup API has no access
 to them at all, which is correct. Showing a customer the lot number on their own line is not
 the same thing as exposing the ledger, but the boundary has to be drawn explicitly in code rather than
 assumed, or someone will later "helpfully" widen it.

 Recommendation

 Show the customer their own lot numbers and link each to the public released-lot page. Read it through a narrow query that returns only lines belonging to the signed-in account, never through the movement ledger. Write the rule down next to the one that already protects the lookup API.

 10.4 Reorder
 to build 

 Independent researchers buy the same compound in the same pack size on a rhythm. Reorder is the single
 highest-return thing on this page commercially, and it is a small build because the order already
 holds everything needed to reconstruct itself.

 What reorder must re-check, not copy

 Price. Never carry the old price forward. Totals are immutable per order by design; a reorder is a new offer at current prices.

 Lot. The original lot is probably gone or withdrawn. Reorder allocates from whatever is released now.

 Availability. The pack size may no longer exist.

 Attestation version. If the wording has changed since their last order, re-prompt.

 Their standing. A suspended account reordering should fail the same guard as a new order.

 Recommendation

 Build it as "start a new order from this one" — it pre-fills the cart and the address and then runs the full normal checkout including every guard. Do not build a one-click repeat that bypasses checks; the guards in 5.2 and 5.3 are the whole point and a reorder path that skips them quietly undoes them.

 Saved addresses (10.2) are the dependency. Build those first and reorder becomes almost free.

 10.5 Their documents — pinned to the order, for good
 defect found 

 The requirement is exact: a customer coming back in two years must get the certificate that shipped
 with their order — not a later revision, not the certificate for whatever lot you happen to be
 shipping now. As the schema stands today that is not what happens, and a reorder is the case
 that breaks it. 

 The defect, precisely

 issuedDocuments binds a COA with subjectType = 'lot' and
 subjectId = lotId . So "the COA for this order" can only be answered by walking
 order → line → lot → the current non-superseded COA for that lot . That last step is a live
 lookup, and it is where the record stops being historical.

 Two ways it goes wrong:

 A correction rewrites history. The lot's COA is re-issued — a transcription fix, a re-test, an added panel. supersededById is set, the live lookup now returns v2, and the customer's two-year-old order silently shows a document they never received.

 A reorder contaminates the old order. The customer reorders; the new order ships from a different lot with a different certificate. Any view that resolves documents by product rather than by order line will show the newest certificate against every past purchase of that compound. This is the exact failure you asked about.

 What is already right, and makes the fix small

 issuedDocuments.sha256 stores a SHA-256 of the issued bytes. Any pinned document is byte-verifiable forever.

 documentNumber is unique for all time and never reused.

 objectKey is uniquely indexed, so an issued file has its own immutable storage slot.

 supersededById , supersededAt and supersedeReason already model correction-by-supersession rather than overwrite — which is the same principle the lot records use.

 Everything needed for a permanent record exists. What is missing is the pin.

 The fix — pin at fulfilment, resolve only through the pin

 1. Pin the document to the order line. At the moment a line is allocated to a lot
 and shipped, write coaDocumentId (the issuedDocuments.id ) and
 coaSha256 onto the order line. Do the same for the SDS in force that day. The pin is
 written once and never updated.

 2. The customer view reads the pin and nothing else. No query in the customer
 document path may select "current COA for lot X" or "COA for product Y". If the pin is absent the page
 says the record is unavailable — it does not fall back to a live lookup, because a plausible wrong
 document is worse than an honest gap.

 3. Never delete or overwrite an issued object. Supersession writes a new object and
 a new row; the superseded file stays in storage permanently. Put that in the R2 lifecycle rules, not
 just in a convention.

 4. Show a revision without swapping to it. When a pinned document has been
 superseded, show the pinned one as the primary — "Certificate issued with order NX-260908-0001" — and
 beneath it, clearly secondary: "a revised certificate for this lot was issued on 4 March 2027."
 The customer chooses. The page never decides for them.

 5. Add supersededById to lotDocuments . It has
 supersededAt but no pointer to the replacement, so the chain is broken at the lot-document
 level even though issuedDocuments models it correctly.

 The acceptance test to write

 Issue COA v1 for lot L. Ship order A from lot L. Supersede with COA v2. Ship order B from lot L.
 Then assert: order A returns v1 and its stored sha256 matches the bytes served; order B returns v2;
 neither order's view changes if v3 is issued tomorrow. Run it against a real supersession, not a
 mocked one.

 Until that test passes, treat the document locker as unbuilt — the whole point of it is that it
 cannot be wrong.

 What the locker then contains

 Every invoice and packing slip, by number.

 The exact COA that shipped with each order line, with its accession number so they can verify it with the testing lab directly rather than trusting you.

 The SDS in force on the shipment date, plus the current one where it has changed — both labelled, never substituted.

 Their own attestation record, with the version they accepted.

 The accession number is the detail that matters most. Showing it says you expect to be checked,
 which is the most credible thing a supplier in this category can say — and it costs nothing, because
 the field is already on the lot record.

 10.6 Recall reachability — the reason this is launch-critical
 the real case 

 A lot fails a retest, or a supplier discloses a problem, or a customer reports something wrong. You
 hold the lot and open a case. Then you have to answer one question: who received material from
 this lot, and how do I reach them today? 

 The recall workflow is built — containment, consignee identification, communication, action tracking,
 effectiveness and controlled closure. It has never been rehearsed against real data, and it depends
 entirely on the customer record being complete and reachable. A guest order with a typo'd email and no
 phone number is a consignee you cannot notify.

 What makes a consignee unreachable today

 Phone is optional at checkout, so many records will have email only.

 Email is unverified on the guest path — a typo is indistinguishable from a real address.

 Domain mail is unauthenticated, so even a correct address may not receive your notice.

 No second contact route and no record of a failed notification attempt.

 Recommendation

 Treat email verification as a recall requirement rather than an account feature — that reframing is what justifies the friction to a team worried about conversion. Verify the address before the order ships, not before it is placed, so nothing is blocked at the point of sale.

 Then run the mock recall against seeded customer records and count how many you could actually reach. That number is the honest measure of whether this chapter is done.

 10.7 Segmentation, and the line you cannot cross
 rules first 

 Once you can see what people bought, the obvious next step is to group them and market accordingly.
 Some of that is ordinary business. Some of it manufactures evidence against you, and the distinction is
 sharp enough to write as a rule.

 Segment Allowed? Why 

 By compound purchased Yes A factual record of what you sold. 

 By order frequency or value Yes Ordinary commercial fact. Useful for terms and stock planning. 

 By tier or institution Yes Stated by the customer. 

 By lot received Yes — required This is the recall query. It is the most important one you have. 

 By research setting, as they stated it Yes Their own answer from a fixed list. Descriptive, never gating. 

 By inferred purpose or outcome No Creates a written record that you believed the material was for human use. 

 By residential address No — dropped See below. A home lab is a research setting, not a risk signal. 

 Campaigns framed on an effect No A claim in an email is a claim. The swap test applies to marketing exactly as it applies to the site. 

 Correction — the residential-address flag comes out entirely

 Chapter 5 originally proposed capturing residential-versus-commercial from the address validator and
 recording it. On your direction that is withdrawn , and on reflection you are right for
 a better reason than convenience.

 Independent researchers increasingly work from home labs and home offices — that is the whole premise
 of the buyer you are selling to. A field that separates them from institutional addresses is a field
 whose only possible use is to treat one group as more suspicious than the other. And it fails this
 chapter's own rule from 10.1: do not record theories about behaviour. A residential flag is precisely
 the kind of field a plaintiff would point at and ask what you did with it.

 So: no capture, no flag, no restriction, and no shipping rule that turns on it. Where a carrier needs
 a residential indicator to rate a parcel correctly, that is a shipping API parameter resolved at
 label time — not a stored attribute of the customer.

 Recommendation — dropdowns, not free text

 Replace the free-text institution field with a fixed list the customer picks from. A dropdown gives
 you data you can actually group, it stops people typing things into your records that you would rather
 not hold, and it makes the home-lab researcher a first-class option rather than an unclassifiable
 outlier:

 University or academic institution

 Hospital or clinical research facility

 Commercial or industrial laboratory

 Contract research organisation

 Government or national laboratory

 Independent or home laboratory

 Other research setting

 Alongside it, an optional free-text organisation name for anyone who wants to give one — needed for
 an invoice anyway. Neither field gates anything.

 Then segment on what was bought and when, never on why. The honest version also performs better:
 "your lot's retest is due" and "this compound is back in stock" are genuinely useful to a researcher
 and carry no claim at all.

 10.8 Who counts as a researcher — and why you should stop counting heads
 corrected 

 Rev. 1 of this chapter opened by saying individual researchers are individuals, so privacy obligations
 apply differently than they would to a university purchase order. That framing was wrong and
 you were right to push back on it. It quietly made you classify every customer as a person or
 an organisation, which is a determination you do not want to make, cannot make reliably, and gain
 nothing from making.

 The qualifying question is one question

 Are you a researcher? That is the whole gate. Not "are you an individual or a
 group", not "how big is your lab", not "is this a home address". A sole researcher with a bench in a
 converted garage and a four-person startup lab are the same customer to you, and the difference between
 them is none of your business.

 Scale is a descriptive attribute, captured from the 10.7 dropdown because it is useful for stocking
 and pack sizes — never a gate, never a tier, never a permission.

 What this changes in the tier design

 Chapter 5 proposed replacing consumer with independent_researcher . On this
 correction, drop the "independent": the tier is simply researcher . "Independent" is exactly
 the individual-versus-group judgement you have just said you do not want to encode, and it would sit in
 the schema making that judgement on every row.

 tier: 'researcher' — everyone who passes the attestation. One class of customer.

 Commercial arrangement is a separate field, not a tier: self-serve checkout, or purchase order with net terms. A one-person lab can be on net terms; a big institution can pay by card. Those are billing facts, not customer classes.

 Research setting from the 10.7 dropdown — descriptive, never gating.

 This is simpler than what you have now and it removes a determination rather than adding one.

 Consent, retention and deletion — one standard for everyone

 The same logic resolves the privacy question. Rather than deciding who gets individual-level
 protection, apply the stronger standard to every customer record. It costs almost
 nothing, it is what a home-lab researcher would expect, and it means you never have to defend a
 classification you did not want to make.

 Consent. Transactional mail needs none. Anything else needs a recorded opt-in with a timestamp and an unsubscribe that actually works.

 Retention. Two clocks, written as two rules. Order and lot records are the traceability chain and a recall depends on them — they are kept. Marketing data is not, and should expire.

 Deletion. A customer can ask you to delete their data; you cannot delete the distribution record without breaking the lot chain. Say plainly up front what is kept as a regulatory record and why. Decide it now, not during the first request.

 Guest records. A guest contact is an order snapshot, not a verified identity. Say so in the profile policy so nobody later treats one as a confirmed customer.

 10.9 What staff should see on a customer
 to build 

 Fatima picks up an email from someone who ordered three months ago. Today she has an orders list she can
 filter. What she needs is one page.

 The customer page

 Who they are, their tier, their stated research context, and when they last ordered.

 Their attestation: which version, accepted when. Flagged if out of date.

 Every order with status, and every lot they hold.

 Open cases, past complaints, returns and refunds.

 Notes, attributed and dated.

 Whether they are reachable — email verified, phone present, last notice delivered or bounced.

 Who sees what

 Customer data is a permissions question and the current three-role matrix does not answer it cleanly
 (finding F-03 in the main register). Fulfilment needs the address; quality needs the lot-to-consignee
 link for a recall; finance needs payment history. Nobody needs all three routinely.
