 Three states, in sequence, and only the middle one is a problem you can avoid.

 When What a searcher finds

 at cutover Every one of those URLs 404s. The new build has no sitemap, so there is nothing to replace them with. Findable for the wrong thing, then briefly not findable at all. 

 after Whatever you tell search engines about the new site — which today is nothing. 

 Record the ranking drop as a decision

 The new site is deliberately not written for the query the old one ranked for. A drop is the intended
 consequence of repositioning, not a defect — but it should be written down and accepted by the members
 beforehand, so nobody treats it as something gone wrong three weeks later.

 1.2 The redirect map, decided per URL
 build 

 Every live path needs a decision. A blanket redirect to the homepage is the worst option —
 it looks like an error to a searcher and search engines treat it as a soft 404 anyway.

 Old path Do Why 

 /privacy-policy 301 → /legal/privacy Same document, new home. 

 /shipping-policy 301 → /legal/shipping Same. 

 /about, /faq 301 → /about, /faq Direct equivalents exist. 

 /shop 301 → /catalog The closest thing, and it carries whatever authority the page has. 

 /product/<slug> 301 or 410 Product by product. A product you still sell redirects to its new page. A product you do not gets 410 Gone , not 404 — 410 tells search engines to drop it deliberately rather than keep retrying. 

 /cart, /checkout, /my-account, /customer-dashboard 410 Genuinely gone. They should never have been indexed. 

 /shop-2, /accessibility-statement 410 Gone.

 Pull the full URL list from sitemap_index.xml and from Search Console, not from memory.

 1.3 The sitemap, and what belongs in it
 missing 

 app/robots.ts exists. There is no sitemap route, so /sitemap.xml 
 returns 404. For a business whose entire discovery strategy is long-tail chemical identity, that is the
 most consequential missing file on the site.

 What goes in

 Every published product page.

 Every released lot page. These are the pages nobody else has — a per-lot certificate page is genuinely unique content and it multiplies honestly as you release lots.

 The chemical class index pages.

 Documentation, lot lookup, SDS index, FAQ, about, contact, legal.

 What stays out

 Quarantined, held, rejected or withdrawn lot pages. The public lookup already returns released lots only and 404s the rest — the sitemap must not leak what the API deliberately hides.

 Account, cart, order and manage paths.

 Any page that renders a price to a signed-out visitor, if the members land on a gated position.

 1.4 The long tail that actually converts
 the strategy 

 A researcher looking for material searches the way a chemist thinks: a CAS number, a
 sequence, a molecular formula, a purity spec, a supplier plus a lot number. These are low-volume,
 low-competition, and the people typing them are buying rather than browsing.

 Pages that earn traffic honestly

 Chemical identity depth on every product — IUPAC name, CAS, related CAS forms, sequence in one- and three-letter, formula, MW, exact mass, SMILES, InChI key, PubChem CID. You already store every one of these fields, and most competitors publish none of them.

 Per-lot COA pages — searchable by lot number and accession number. The accession number is the testing lab’s own reference, which means a customer can verify with the lab rather than trust you.

 Analytical method content — how purity is determined, what HPLC at a stated wavelength does and does not tell you, why net peptide content differs from purity. This is the content that makes you credible to the person with a budget.

 Pack-size and format pages — multiplies page count honestly rather than by writing thin variants.

 Expect thousands of qualified visits, not the hundreds of thousands the traffic leaders get. Their
 volume comes from dosing calculators and protocol content, which is exactly the content that draws
 enforcement.

 1.5 The adjacency trap
 guardrail 

 The obvious way to grow search traffic in this category is to publish educational content
 about the compounds. That is the single most dangerous thing you could build, and the reason is
 structural rather than about wording.

 Adjacency is the theory of the case

 In every warning letter FDA has issued here, the evidence was the relationship between a
 claim and an order button. A research library that links to a product page turns an article into
 promotional labelling for the product it links to. The article does not have to say anything
 outrageous; it has to sit next to the buy button.

 If both must exist, they must be genuinely separate — different section, no cross-links from
 article to product, no “shop this compound” module, no shared navigation that treats them as one
 funnel.

 The safer growth path

 Write about your methods, not the molecules . How to read a certificate of analysis.
 What an accession number is and why you should check it. Why net peptide content matters. What a
 manufacturer name and address on a COA tells you and what its absence tells you. This content is
 genuinely useful, ranks for buying-intent queries, cannot be read as a claim about any compound, and
 positions you against exactly the competitors whose certificates do not carry those fields.

 docs/content/how-to-read-a-certificate-of-analysis.md already exists. That is the model.

 1.6 Measuring it without fooling yourself
 set up 

 Traffic is the wrong number for this business. A thousand visitors who cannot buy are worth
 less than forty who can, and chasing volume is what drags a site in this category toward the content
 that gets it a letter.

 What to watch

 Search Console verified for the new property before the cutover, so you have a before picture.

 Impressions and clicks on identity queries — CAS numbers, sequences, formulas — tracked separately from brand queries.

 Lot-page and COA-lookup usage. A customer verifying a certificate is the strongest quality signal you can get.

 Quote and contact submissions per hundred identity-query visits. That is the real conversion number.
